A certified thermal twin for power transformers

A transformer's real limit is the hot spot inside its winding, and nobody can put a sensor there while it's in service. So utilities rate for the worst case and leave capacity sitting idle, while AI data centers and new solar and wind wait years for a new unit. I'm building a twin that sees inside.

A multi-fidelity, physics-informed neural operator, trained on 3,200 coarse and 320 fine conjugate heat-transfer simulations, predicts the full temperature field through the winding in 40.8 ms. It only learns the correction on top of the IEEE C57.91 standard, never the physics the standard already knows. A second layer turns that into a rating an operator can sign: interval bounds on the hot spot over the whole dispatch window, a calibrated margin for model error, and an aging-aware controller that says how much extra load it buys and what that costs in insulation life.

0.542 °Chot-spot error on designs it never saw
40.8 msper full temperature field
0.225 °Ctop-oil error on real transformers, vs 4.15 °C for C57.91

The honest gap: the hot spot is only checked against my own simulator. Everything next is built to close that.

  • 3-D, at scale. Keep thousands of cheap 2-D runs and add a few dozen 3-D sector runs through the oil ducts, then show the mix beats either alone. The thermal fields go out as an open dataset, because none exists publicly.
  • A real measurement. A tabletop oil-cooled coil with thermocouples first, then a short-circuit heat run on a small distribution transformer with a probe at the predicted hot spot.
  • What it's worth to a grid. Public NYISO load and curtailment data turned into an estimate of how much capacity a certified rating frees across a fleet.
  • Where it's going. Terra NYC, JSHS and ISEF this year, then a journal paper once it's validated against measured hot-spot data.

Certified neural Lyapunov functions for the grid

A learned stability function for grid frequency looked perfect by testing. It passed on 99.98% of sampled states and 200,000 random samples found nothing, but a directed search found violations 6.1% of the time. Testing visits points and a proof has to cover a region. So we put the verifier inside training: CROWN branch and bound finds the counterexamples, and the network trains through the bound until it's verifiable by construction.

Open source: a feasibility check for grid foundation models (OpenGridFM)
2026 – Present
Grid foundation models like GENCO answer a power-flow question in milliseconds, but nobody can act on an answer without knowing it's right. My check warm-starts Newton from the model's prediction and certifies it only if a true solution sits right next to it. On GENCO's published outage predictions for the 2,000-bus Texas grid, it certified 609 of 989 cases with no wrong answer passed, and the warm start halves Newton's iterations. I'm developing it with the OpenGridFM maintainers at IBM Research and Hydro-Québec.

Spool: CAD in, tuned robot out
2026 · in development
Every FTC and FRC team tunes its robot by hand, after it's built, one mechanism at a time. Spool starts from the CAD. It pulls mass, inertia and gearing out of the assembly, computes starting feedforward and PIDF gains for the drivetrain, flywheel, turret and arm from motor physics, tunes them in simulation, and exports them straight into Pedro Pathing for FTC and WPILib for FRC. Then one short logged run on the real robot fits the friction CAD can't see. The test robot is my team's FTC bot, FAT MAN.
The AI data-center boom: why it's a control problemArticle  ·  2026 – Present  ·  growing up around the grid, and what I've built for a grid under AI load

My dad has been a grid operating engineer for 35 years. So for me the power grid was never a bill or a wall socket. It was a machine, and the biggest one people have ever built. Every large generator on it spins in lockstep at 60 cycles a second, and every time someone flips something on, the whole thing has to answer within seconds or the frequency starts to slide.

What got me was how much of that answering is done by controllers that were tuned once and left alone. The hardware ages, the loads change, new kinds of generation show up, and the control law mostly stays where it was set.

The data-center boom is a control problem

Now the grid is being asked to take on something it wasn't planned for. AI data centers show up as huge blocks of load, they show up fast, and they want to connect where the wires were sized for a different decade. A training campus can swing tens of megawatts in seconds as thousands of accelerators compute and then sync together. Most of the public conversation is about generation, how many plants we need. Studying interconnection and talking to the people who run it, I've come to think the harder question is dynamic. Can the system stay stable while it changes this fast, with learned components making more of the calls?

What I've built on it

  • A proved ramp limit for a data center's UPS. A battery-backed uninterruptible power supply between the campus and the grid, with a proof that for every load profile in range the grid sees a bounded ramp and the battery never runs out, plus a sizing rule within 1.43× of what any controller could need. Paper · figures and proof. Under review, IEEE PES GM 2027.
  • A fleet of those UPS batteries as grid support. With ±10 MW of headroom per campus on the Kundur two-area model, verified inter-area damping goes from 0.024 to 0.106. Paper. Under review, IEEE PES GM 2027.
  • A certified screen for interconnection queues. How much a bus can take in 1.4 ms instead of hundreds of AC power flows, with a certificate that says which answers to trust and which to send to a full study. Presented to PJM's Applied Innovation team. Paper · PJM slides. Under review, IEEE PES GM 2027.
  • How much regulation flexible AI compute can promise. Two theorems that bound it, 17.32 MW certified against a 17.34 MW provable ceiling on a 40-job pool, where naive headroom accounting overpromises by 53%. Note.

Sampling isn't proof

All of it comes back to the same thing I learned with Prof. Cui. Testing visits points and a proof has to cover a region. The grid is going to get learned controllers whether anyone's ready or not, because it's the only way to keep up. I want them to come with proofs.

Operator · the Index of Operations
2026 · presented to a16z Alpha
A machine is parts that pass energy to each other. The Index of Operations gives every part its own axes, the stator, the winding, the bearing, the housing, and reads the shape they draw together as the machine runs. When a connection stops conserving what crosses it, something is wrong there. On real physical AI datasets it catches faults that raw sensor features miss, and it barely moves when the sensors are recalibrated, which is where most monitoring quietly breaks. The control side, a Lyapunov shield that keeps the shape from moving the wrong way, is in simulation so far, and a certified controller that ages with the machine is under review at L4DC 2027. I presented Operator to Joe Garcia and the a16z Alpha Fellowship team in November 2026.
