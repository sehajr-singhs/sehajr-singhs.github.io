I build learned control for physical machines that comes with proofs.
I'm the founder of Guerilla and a control and dynamics engineer in New York. Guerilla is building the Index of Operations, one representation that reads a machine as the shape its physical parts draw together and uses that shape to monitor it, model its dynamics and control it. The goal is the control layer for the machine age: the grid, the factory floor and the robots that come next.
I also work with Prof. Wenqi Cui's group at NYU Tandon ECE on certified stability for learned grid controllers, where my first-author paper was accepted at NeurIPS 2026's Tackling Climate Change with ML workshop, and I intern on data-center grid infrastructure.

Guerilla · the Index of Operations building
2026 · Founder
The Index of Operations is an index of the physical operators every machine is made of, like motors, pumps, joints and thermal masses, connected where they pass energy and force. Build a machine from it and its telemetry becomes a shape. That shape doesn't move when sensors are recalibrated (0.749 to 0.736 AUROC on ESA's OPS-SAT satellite, where raw features fall to 0.487), it beats the published OPS-SAT baseline (0.911 to 0.951), and the same signal that detects a fault gates the controller, cutting tracking error 8.2× under a 5× actuator fault in simulation. From one index it goes two ways: a world model of machines, and tiny models that run at the edge and retrofit the installed industrial base.

What world models throw awayMachines break at the low level first. A model that predicts in a compressed latent can't see it coming.

I think the deepest unsolved problem in physical AI is representation. Not scale, not compute. How do you take continuous sensor data off a real machine and put it into a mathematical space that keeps the physics?

The best world models right now answer that with compression. They encode the scene into a smaller latent, predict how the latent moves, and treat the fine detail as noise. For a lot of the world that's the right call. If you want to know where a thrown ball lands, you don't need the stitching on the ball.

Machines are different, because machines break at exactly the level compression throws away. A bearing starts to wear. A winding runs a few degrees hot. A joint picks up a little backlash. None of that changes the big picture at first. What changes is how two parts relate, a small break in the energy that should pass cleanly from one to the next. By the time it shows up at the macro level it's not a warning anymore, it's a failure.

A model that predicts in a compressed latent can't see the fault coming, because the fault lives in what it compressed.

Keep the low level, on its own axes

So I went the other way. The Index of Operations doesn't squash a machine into one latent. It splits it into the physical operators it's made of, gives each one its own axes, and joins them where they trade energy, mass, heat or force. Then it watches whether each joint keeps conserving what flows across it.

The payoff shows up in the most boring test there is. On ESA's OPS-SAT satellite telemetry, recalibrate the sensors and a detector on raw features drops from 0.919 to 0.487 AUROC. That's a coin flip, for a change that has nothing to do with the satellite's health. Reading conservation at the joints, it goes 0.749 to 0.736. The machine didn't change, so the representation didn't either.

A robot with no hands

There's a thought experiment I keep coming back to. Put a robot with two rounded stubs for arms in front of a keyboard and tell it to type. A policy trained on video of human hands has nothing to copy, because nothing in its data looks like its body. A model that indexes its own body as operators can treat it as geometry. The stub covers three keys, so tilt until the contact patch covers one, feel for slip where it touches, correct. A new body is a new shape from the same index, not a new dataset.

IOO can't do that yet. The control results are in simulation and I still build each operator by hand. But I'm pretty sure about the direction. The world models that end up running real machines won't be the ones that watched the most video. They'll be the ones that know what the machine is made of.

Invariance you build, not invariance you learnWhy I stopped asking optimizers to discover physics I already know.

There's a habit in machine learning I used to have too. You want a model that doesn't care about something, like which sensor it's reading or how that sensor was calibrated, so you add a loss term and hope the optimizer learns not to care. Domain-adversarial training is the cleanest version of this. Train the features so a second network can't tell the domains apart, and in theory the features become invariant.

In practice it holds up in the lab and falls apart in the field. I ran it on the same recalibration test I use for everything. Given labels and target-domain data, a domain-adversarial network scored 0.983 in-domain on turbofan engines and then dropped 0.41 AUROC once the sensors were rescaled. On satellite telemetry it dropped 0.36. It learned the invariance on the data it saw, and the field gave it data it hadn't seen.

Put it in the representation

The thing is, I already know the invariance I want. Recalibrating a sensor multiplies and shifts one channel. It doesn't change the machine. So instead of asking the optimizer to discover that, I built it into what the model reads. IOO measures how much each physical interface stops conserving, normalized by magnitude, and under that normalization any per-channel rescale cancels exactly. I checked it numerically to 1e-14, and it's provable, not just observed.

Same test, same drift. IOO's slip went from 0.749 to 0.736 on satellites, and the drop on engines was 0.0012.

The optimizer can't recover what the representation never lost.

The catch, and why it's a feature

Invariance has a cost, and I'd rather say it than have someone find it. The property that makes the shape immune to recalibration also makes it blind to slow level drift, and slow level drift is what real wear looks like. Raw statistics catch that and shape doesn't, so the two are complements, not rivals. That's fine with me, because it tells me exactly what each piece is for.

The bigger lesson is about where physics should live. If you know a symmetry, don't spend data teaching it. Build it in, and spend the data on what you don't know yet.

One shape, from monitoring to controlThe signal that tells you something's wrong should be the same one that fixes it.

Most industrial AI stacks are split down the middle. One model watches the machine and raises alarms. A separate controller runs the machine, usually tuned once at commissioning. They don't share a representation, so when the monitor sees something, a human has to translate it into what the controller should do. That translation is slow, and on the grid or a production line slow is expensive.

I wanted one object that does both. In the Index of Operations, the thing that detects a fault is the conservation slip at an interface between two operators. A healthy interface conserves what crosses it, and a failing one stops. That slip is also exactly what a controller needs to know, which part is misbehaving and by how much.

Close the loop with the same signal

So I used it as a gate. The controller runs model predictive control on the IOO graph, and adaptation only turns on at an interface whose slip says it stopped conserving. In simulation, with a 5× drop in actuator gain, slip-gated control had 8.2× lower tracking error than the same controller without the gate. With dropouts and sensor noise thrown in, steady-state error was 11.4× lower.

The part I like most is what happens when nothing's wrong. The gate never opens, so healthy control is bit-identical to the plain controller. Adaptation costs nothing where it isn't needed, and that's the property you need before anyone lets a learned component near real hardware.

Monitoring and control aren't two products. They're two readings of the same shape.

Where it has to go next

All of this is still simulation, a motor and a flywheel on a two-node graph, and there's no stability certificate yet tying the gate to closed-loop robustness. That second gap is the one I'm most equipped to close, because it's the same Lyapunov machinery from my work on certified grid controllers. The first gap closes on a bench, with one frozen controller across a physical actuator swap. That's the next 90 days.

Retrofit the machine ageThe biggest market for physical AI is the machines that already exist.

When people say physical AI they usually mean new things, humanoids and robotaxis and dark factories. Those matter. But most of the machines that will exist in 2035 are already installed today. Motors, pumps, compressors, turbines, transformers. They'll run for decades, and almost none of them get any smarter after the day they're commissioned.

Not by inventing new motors, but by getting existing systems to understand themselves. I wrote some version of that line in my notes a long time ago, and it's still the most underrated opportunity I know.

Why most AI can't retrofit

Retrofitting is hostile to the way most models are built. Every unit is a little different, so a model trained on one doesn't transfer to the next. Sensors get swapped and recalibrated, so features drift for reasons that have nothing to do with health. And there's no labeled failure data, because the whole point is that failures are rare. A model that needs millions of examples per machine never pays for itself on a pump.

Why shape can

IOO works on the telemetry machines already stream, and it reads relationships instead of raw levels, so it doesn't care which sensor brand is on the shaft. The models are graphs of tens of nodes, so they run on a CPU next to the machine. And it doesn't start from zero. A pump and a motor share operators, so the index that learned one already knows a lot about the other.

That's the flywheel. Every machine IOO reads adds operators and interfaces to the index, and every new machine built from known operators starts with a prior instead of an empty model. The data flywheel in most of physical AI runs on more hours of video. This one runs on structure.

What's not done

Right now I hand-build each operator and the catalog is mostly rotating machinery, so the honest claim is a method that works across satellites, engines, a hexapod and a cruise benchmark, not yet a catalog that covers a factory. Learning operators from data is the step that turns this from a method into a company. That's what I'm building next.

The biggest machine we've ever builtGrowing up around the grid, and why the data-center boom is a control problem.

My dad has been a grid operating engineer for 35 years. So for me the power grid was never a bill or a wall socket. It was a machine, and the biggest one people have ever built. Every large generator on it spins in lockstep at 60 cycles a second, and every time someone flips something on, the whole thing has to answer within seconds or the frequency starts to slide.

What got me was how much of that answering is done by controllers that were tuned once and left alone. The hardware ages, the loads change, new kinds of generation show up, and the control law mostly stays where it was set. So I started writing code and running experiments on anything I thought could make it more modern from a control and dynamics view. That turned into research, and the research turned into everything I work on now.

The data-center boom is a control problem

Now the grid is being asked to take on something it wasn't planned for. AI data centers show up as huge blocks of load, they show up fast, and they want to connect where the wires were sized for a different decade. Most of the public conversation is about generation, how many plants we need. Studying interconnection and talking to the people who run it, I've come to think the harder question is dynamic. Can the system stay stable while it changes this fast, with learned components making more of the calls?

Sampling isn't proof

That question is why I work on certified control. With Prof. Wenqi Cui at NYU I took a learned stability function for grid frequency that looked perfect by testing. It passed on 99.98% of sampled states, and 200,000 random samples found nothing. A directed search found violations 6.1% of the time. Testing visits points and a proof has to cover a region. Closing that gap, using the verifier to find counterexamples and then training through its bound, is our NeurIPS workshop paper.

The grid is going to get learned controllers whether anyone's ready or not, because it's the only way to keep up. I want them to come with proofs.
