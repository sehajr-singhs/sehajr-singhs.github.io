Certify or defer: fast, provably safe screening for grid interconnection

New solar, storage and data centers now wait about five years to connect to the U.S. grid, and the bottleneck is the interconnection study. Grid operators like PJM study requests in clusters, because projects that each fit on their own can overload the grid together. Fast screens that judge requests one at a time, or with a linear model, miss that interaction.

I'm building a screen that pairs a learned model with a guarantee. A network that reads both the grid's structure and the set of requests predicts how much a cluster can really take, and a statistical certificate turns that into a capacity that holds at a chosen confidence level. Anything it can't vouch for goes to the full AC study, so it never replaces the study, it decides which cases need it. I reproduced PJM's cluster-study optimization exactly, and I presented the method to PJM's Applied Innovation team.

34%median share of the sum of its requests' individual capacities that a cluster can jointly take (IEEE 118-bus)
1 in 3unsafe clusters a linear screen calls safe
0 of 989wrong answers passed when certifying a published AI model on the 2,000-bus Texas grid

Results so far are on IEEE test grids and the public Texas synthetic grid, not on a utility's real network model. What's next: hundreds of clusters on the 2,000-bus Texas grid shaped by public queue data, a proven bound on how often the screen can call an unsafe cluster safe, and a check against the outcomes PJM published for its first cluster cycle.

Certified neural Lyapunov functions for the grid

A learned stability function for grid frequency looked perfect by testing. It passed on 99.98% of sampled states and 200,000 random samples found nothing, but a directed search found violations 6.1% of the time. Testing visits points and a proof has to cover a region. So we put the verifier inside training: CROWN branch and bound finds the counterexamples, and the network trains through the bound until it's verifiable by construction.

Open source: a feasibility check for grid foundation models (OpenGridFM)
2026 – Present
Grid foundation models like GENCO answer a power-flow question in milliseconds, but nobody can act on an answer without knowing it's right. My check warm-starts Newton from the model's prediction and certifies it only if a true solution sits right next to it. On GENCO's published outage predictions for the 2,000-bus Texas grid, it certified 609 of 989 cases with no wrong answer passed, and the warm start halves Newton's iterations. I'm developing it with the OpenGridFM maintainers at IBM Research and Hydro-Québec.

The AI data-center boom is a people problem tooArticle  ·  2026  ·  who actually decides how fast the grid can grow

The headline numbers are big. One estimate puts the extra electricity AI could use by 2027 at 85 to 134 TWh a year, about what a whole country uses (de Vries, 2023). PJM, the grid operator for 67 million people, came up 6,625 MW short of its reliability target in its December 2025 capacity auction, after its demand forecast jumped 5,250 MW, almost all of it data centers. The same auction cleared only 774 MW of new generation (Howland, 2025).

We've been here before

Data-center demand roughly doubled from 2000 to 2005 (U.S. EPA, 2007), then nearly flattened. U.S. growth fell to about 4% from 2010 to 2014 as servers were virtualized and cooling got better (Shehabi et al., 2016), and global data-center electricity rose only about 6% from 2010 to 2018 while computing grew many times over (Masanet et al., 2020). So demand doesn't automatically follow compute. It's also worth being careful with the forecasts: a review of 258 estimates found only 31% of their sources were peer-reviewed (Mytton & Ashtine, 2022).

The bottleneck has names

What's different now is how hard it is for supply to answer. About 2,600 GW of projects are waiting to connect to U.S. grids, double the whole existing fleet. Waits are up 70% in a decade, and about 80% of requests eventually withdraw (Gorman et al., 2025). PJM's auction hit its price cap for the third straight time, at $16.4 billion, with permitting, financing and gas-turbine shortages all cited (Howland, 2025). And the wiring itself is a labor problem: electrical work is 45 to 70% of a data center's construction cost, and Microsoft's president has called the electrician shortage the number one thing slowing its U.S. expansion (Fore, 2026). Not every effect is bad. U.S. power prices mostly tracked inflation from 2019 to 2024 and fell in real terms in 31 states (Wiser et al., 2025).

The fixes are almost all technical

The best ideas so far treat AI load as something that can move: shift training to when the grid has room (Lin et al., 2024). A 256-GPU cluster in Phoenix cut its power 25% for three hours at peak and still met its service guarantees (Colangelo et al., 2026). That's real, but it doesn't touch the queue, the permit or the shortage of people who can wire a substation. Energy research has a long habit of this: across more than 4,000 articles in three major journals, social science and interviews were badly underused (Sovacool et al., 2014).

The question I'm asking

Every one of those bottlenecks is a decision somebody makes: an interconnection engineer approving a study, an electrician taking a job, a utility planner trusting a forecast, a town board voting on a permit. The technical work I do helps on one side of that: Certify or Defer is aimed at the first decision, the interconnection study. The other side is the people, and that's what my AP Research project asks: how interconnection approvals, electrician supply, utility planning and local permitting decide whether generation can keep pace with AI load in PJM through 2030.

Operator · the Index of Operations
2026 · presented to a16z Alpha
A machine is parts that pass energy to each other. The Index of Operations gives every part its own axes, the stator, the winding, the bearing, the housing, and reads the shape they draw together as the machine runs. When a connection stops conserving what crosses it, something is wrong there. On real physical AI datasets it catches faults that raw sensor features miss, and it barely moves when the sensors are recalibrated, which is where most monitoring quietly breaks. The control side, a Lyapunov shield that keeps the shape from moving the wrong way, is in simulation so far, and a certified controller that ages with the machine is under review at L4DC 2027. I presented Operator to Joe Garcia and the a16z Alpha Fellowship team in November 2026.
